# Brunch: Persuading Users vs Making Them Understand (UX Writing)

**Citation**: Brunch (@c5738bbb3f0c443). "유저를 설득하는 일과 유저를 이해시키는 일은, 다르다" (Persuading users is different from making them understand). 
**Source type**: Article (Korean)
**Location**: `[[raw/web/brunch-c5738bbb3f0c443-44.md]]` / [Original URL](https://brunch.co.kr/@c5738bbb3f0c443/44)

## Summary
A practical reflection on UX writing for banners and CTAs, emphasizing that users scan text in under 0.3 seconds. The author outlines five multiplicative principles for high-converting UX copy: single message, certain reward, light verbs, specific actions, and familiar words. Good UX writing is defined as the precarious intersection between a company's conversion needs and a user's desire for minimal intervention.

## Key claims
- **Users don't read; they scan** in less than 0.3 seconds. The decision is made before the sentence is fully parsed.
- **The 5 Principles of High-Converting UX Writing:**
  1. **Single Message**: Leave only one compelling reason to click. Multiple messages create cognitive overload.
  2. **Certain Reward**: Users often react more strongly to small but certain rewards than to large but uncertain maximums (though this varies by domain/price point).
  3. **Light Verbs (Low Friction)**: Use verbs that don't demand a heavy commitment. E.g., "Check" or "View" instead of "Start" or "Write". This lowers the psychological barrier to entry.
  4. **Specific Actions**: Specificity reduces uncertainty. "Enter name, address, phone number to apply" converts better than a vague "Enter info to apply" because the user knows exactly what the cost of action is.
  5. **Familiar Words**: Foreign or unfamiliar words introduce a 0.1-second processing delay, which is enough to make a user scroll past. Familiar words respect the user's short attention span.
- **The principles are multiplicative, not additive.** If any one of the five elements is close to zero, the overall effectiveness (CTR) crashes.
- **Final Validation**: Always read the copy out loud. Visual smoothness can hide awkward phrasing that auditory reading exposes.

## Useful examples
- Changing a heavy CTA verb like "Proceed" to a lighter verb like "Check" multiplied the CTR.
- Specifying the exact input required ("Enter name and phone number") rather than a generic prompt reduces anxiety and increases conversion.

## Constraints / caveats
- Based on A/B testing in a specific (likely Korean consumer app) context. The rule of "small certainty over large uncertainty" might not apply to high-ticket items (like appliances) where a "Up to $300 off" hook might work better.
- Practitioner-level insights; not peer-reviewed academic research.

## Design implications
- Banners and CTAs must be designed for 0.3-second scanning, treating reading as a secondary or non-existent behavior.
- Lowering the commitment level of the CTA verb is a crucial design lever for unengaged or low-trust users.

## Tensions
- **Lightness vs. Specificity**: Using a "light" verb (low friction) seems contradictory to providing specific, detailed instructions (which adds text). However, they operate on different levels: the verb reduces commitment anxiety, while the specific instruction removes uncertainty.

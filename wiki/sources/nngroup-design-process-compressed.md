# Design Process Isn't Dead, It's Compressed (NN/G)

## Summary

NN/G rebuts the "throw out the process / trust your intuition / skip research / start building" argument (associated with Jenny Wen at Anthropic) by reframing it: what looks like skipping the process is really **process compression** — experienced designers run an internalized, nonlinear version of discovery → ideation → evaluation faster, guided by accumulated experience. The double diamond and design thinking were never literal checklists; they are risk-management scaffolding. "What gets called intuition is really process, compressed and internalized." AI tools (vibecoding/AI prototyping) accelerate *and democratize* this compression. But intuition cannot replace process: it is unreliable for juniors, lacks accountability for stakeholder alignment, is unsafe in regulated/high-stakes domains, and carries undetected bias. Solution-first design works only in narrow, mature, high-maturity contexts and is distorted by survivorship bias. The durable skill is **process literacy**: deliberately matching process to problem and knowing the risk of skipping it. Speed does not remove the need for problem framing.

## Key Claims

- "Skipping the process" is really compressing it — running the stages faster and nonlinearly, often inside established patterns. The frameworks are simplified representations of universal problem-solving stages, not procedures to follow rigidly.
- **Intuition = internalized, compressed process.** It was built *by* the process its advocates dismiss; it is not an alternative to it.
- AI/vibecoding accelerates compression and democratizes it — less experienced practitioners can now run the loop faster, as experts always have.
- Intuition does not replace process for four reasons: (1) not viable for juniors lacking accumulated knowledge; (2) lacks accountability ("what evidence backs this up?"); (3) unsafe in regulated/high-stakes domains (health, finance, government, accessibility); (4) carries bias that gets *harder* to detect the deeper the intuition.
- Solution-first design suits only a narrow slice (mature product space, sophisticated users, discrete differentiation, high org/UX maturity); the cited wins reflect survivorship bias.
- Speed of execution does not diminish the importance of problem framing — you must still know what you're trying to fix.
- The real skill is **process literacy**: choosing the right process for the problem deliberately, and not pretending you're process-free when you're just applying it differently.

## Useful Examples

- **Process-compression illustration:** a seasoned designer on a mature, competitive consumer product who says "I just started building" is running an internalized loop within well-tested patterns — not abandoning process.
- **Regulated-domain contrast:** "Skipping research on a medical device UI is not the same as skipping research on a whiteboarding feature" — a clean heuristic for when process is a safeguard vs. optional.
- **Survivorship-bias example:** Jenny Wen's solution-first examples are successful features of a well-resourced AI company; the failed solution-first experiments (impressive prototypes that never resonated, shipped features with no adoption) are invisible.
- The four "intuition fails" categories work as a ready-made checklist for deciding whether intuition-led work is appropriate on a given project.

## Design Implications

- Use "process compression" as the framing when defending research/discovery to teams under speed pressure — it reframes the debate from "process vs. no process" to "internalized vs. explicit process."
- Gate the intuition-led approach behind the four failure modes: for juniors, accountability-heavy environments, regulated/high-stakes domains, or low-maturity orgs, keep explicit process and artifacts.
- Treat solution-first / vibecoding as valid only when org+UX maturity, user sophistication, and pattern maturity are all high; otherwise insist on lightweight problem framing first.
- Adopt "process literacy" as a team competency: a deliberate match of method depth to problem risk, rather than a fixed six-week discovery for everything.

## Tensions

- **Speed vs. rigor.** AI compresses the loop, but the article insists uncertainty/risk that process mitigates does not disappear — the faster you go, the more the "does our work map to the problem?" question matters.
- **Expert intuition vs. democratization.** AI lets juniors run the loop fast, yet the piece warns juniors lack the knowledge that makes intuition reliable — a direct tension the article does not fully resolve.
- **NN/G vs. Wen/Anthropic.** A practitioner-school disagreement about whether solution-first is a general method or a context-bound one; this source is one side.

## Open Questions

- How do you operationalize "process literacy" as a teachable rubric rather than a slogan?
- At what measurable thresholds of org/UX maturity does solution-first stop amplifying risk?
- Does AI-accelerated compression actually transfer *judgment* to juniors, or just *speed* — and can the difference be measured?
- How should agentic systems that "remember and decide on our behalf" be held to process accountability?

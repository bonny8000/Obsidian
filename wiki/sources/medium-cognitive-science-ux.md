# Medium: Cognitive Science and User Experience

**Citation**: Medium (Design Bootcamp). "Cognitive Science and User Experience".
**Source type**: Article
**Location**: `[[raw/web/medium-cognitive-science-ux-2026-07-20.md]]` / [Original URL](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/cognitive-science-and-user-experience-62d0b995dc1a)

## Summary
This article argues that UX professionals often hold a narrow, heavily quantitative view of UX focused solely on usability testing, while neglecting the foundational role of cognitive and social sciences. It outlines how cognitive science—an interdisciplinary field combining psychology, artificial intelligence, neuroscience, anthropology, philosophy, and linguistics—can deepen our understanding of user behavior, attention, cognitive load, and reasoning, ultimately leading to better, more humanized product experiences.

## Key Claims
- Many transitioned UX professionals mistakenly restrict UX to surface-level redesigns based on usability testing and card sorting, ignoring deeper social science principles.
- The ignorance of cognitive science in UX stems from a lack of secondary research, lack of time, and an over-emphasis on real-time behavioral analytics.
- **Marr's Tri-Level of Analysis** provides a framework for understanding information processing systems (like human vision) across three levels: Computational (what/why), Representation and Algorithmic (how/representations), and Implementation (physical realization).
- Each sub-discipline of cognitive science contributes directly to UX:
  - **Psychology**: Explains emotions, motivations, heuristics, and biases.
  - **Anthropology**: Shows how society and culture shape user experiences.
  - **Linguistics**: Informs UX writing, icon choices, and highlights how language impacts memory and decision-making (Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).
  - **Neuroscience**: Connects biological factors (e.g., cortisol levels) to the user experience.
  - **Philosophy**: Frames questions about ethics, objectivity, and how user ideologies impact experiences.
  - **Artificial Intelligence**: Moves beyond NLP/Speech to incorporate Expert Systems that simulate expert knowledge for better digital products.
- As interfaces evolve beyond point-and-click to AI, AR, and voice, an in-depth understanding of cognitive science becomes even more critical.

## Useful Examples
- **David Marr's Model**: Treating human vision as an information processing system to demonstrate how a phenomenon must be studied from multiple distinct levels to be fully understood.
- **Expert Systems**: Using AI to simulate human expertise within digital products to enhance UX.
- **Linguistics & Icons**: Customizing icons and symbols because linguistic differences (as per the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis) affect nonlinguistic cognitive domains like perception.

## Constraints / Caveats
- The piece serves as a high-level introductory overview rather than an empirical study or rigorous academic framework. 
- It generalizes the knowledge gap among "transitioned UX professionals."

## Design Implications
- UX Research should not only measure "who clicked what" (behavioral analytics) but also understand *why* (cognitive load, memory, attention).
- Design processes must intentionally incorporate cross-disciplinary insights (e.g., checking if a design decision is ethical [philosophy] or checking if language localization affects perception [linguistics]).

## Tensions
- The conflict between the fast-paced, highly quantitative nature of modern digital product development (which relies on quick behavioral analytics) versus the slow, deep nature of cognitive science (which relies on secondary research and deep theoretical frameworks).

## Open Questions
- How can UX teams practically integrate Marr's Tri-Level of Analysis into everyday design sprints?
- What are the most effective ways to measure and apply neuroscientific factors (like cortisol levels) ethically in UX research?

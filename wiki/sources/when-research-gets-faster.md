# Venkat (2026): When Research Gets Faster, Why Do We Need Researchers?

## Citation

Jyothi Venkat, *When Research Gets Faster, Why Do We Need Researchers?*, **Jyothi Venkat's Substack**, 2026-07-27.

**Source type:** Opinion essay. Argument, not evidence — no study data.
**Raw capture:** [[raw/web/jyothiwrites-research-gets-faster-2026-07-28|jyothiwrites-research-gets-faster-2026-07-28]]

## Summary

The essay's move is to relocate the profession's value rather than defend its tasks. AI automated the **middle** of research — transcription, coding, first-pass analysis. What it did not touch is the **front** (deciding what question could change an outcome) and the **back** (making a finding actually own a decision). Researchers remain necessary because organizations are bad at both ends, and neither end is what got automated.

The most useful claim for this wiki is the second-order one: **faster research can expose organizational dysfunction rather than fix it.** If the constraint was never study throughput, removing the throughput constraint just reveals what the real constraint was.

## Key Claims

- **Research begins before data arrives.** The critical work is clarifying the decision, surfacing assumptions, and determining what evidence could realistically change the outcome — rarely automated because it is not a data-processing task.
- **Volume creates noise, not insight.** More studies do not improve decision quality; organizations already struggle to prioritize conflicting signals.
- **Interpretation requires judgment** grounded in business context, product dynamics, and risk tolerance.
- **Research is an organizational system, not a method.** Methodologically sound findings fail when they lack ownership, face competing priorities, or arrive after commitment.
- **Automation without redefinition reduces influence.** Replacing analytical labor while leaving research's strategic function undefined can *lower* impact even as speed rises.

## Useful Examples

The four examples are the essay's strongest content, because each names a study that was methodologically fine and organizationally useless:

- **Interview-transcript analysis tools** — the demonstration case of what AI genuinely does well.
- **Feature-preference testing run after architecture was finalized** — the finding could not change the outcome.
- **Onboarding-abandonment research where causes were already known** — the study answered a settled question.
- **Trust studies conducted ahead of an already-made launch decision** — validation theater.

Every one of these is a *timing or framing* failure, not a rigor failure. That is the essay's argument in concrete form.

**Practical prescriptions:** move from study execution to decision partnering · invest in assumption validation before research begins · track post-launch outcomes to demonstrate business impact · design organizational systems that give findings ownership and accountability.

## Constraints / Caveats

- **No evidence of any kind.** No study, no data, no cases beyond illustrative sketches. This is a well-constructed argument and should be cited as one.
- **The examples are unattributed** — no company, product, or outcome. They read as composites, which limits them to illustration.
- **Silent on the hard parts**, which the author does not claim to address: under-resourced teams, the mechanics of upstream/downstream engagement, and organizational resistance to expanded research influence. The prescriptions therefore say *what* without *how*.
- **Prescription-incentive alignment.** "Researchers are more necessary than ever, at a higher altitude" is the conclusion most flattering to the author's audience. That does not make it wrong — much of this wiki's stronger evidence points the same way — but the essay does not stress-test the opposite case.
- **Does not engage the counter-argument** that decision partnering may need fewer researchers than study execution did. If the work moves upstream, headcount may follow the automated tasks out.

## Design Implications

- **Gate studies on decision-changeability before running them.** "What outcome would this change, and is that outcome still open?" — a question this wiki already operationalizes as the [[wiki/concepts/ux-research/decision-contract|Decision Contract]]. This essay is an independent argument for why that gate matters more as cost falls.
- **Treat cheap research as a prioritization problem, not a capacity win.** When studies become nearly free, the scarce resource becomes stakeholder attention and decision slots.
- **Budget the front and back of the process, not the middle.** If AI compressed analysis, reinvest the time in framing and follow-through rather than in more studies.
- **Instrument post-launch outcomes** as part of research practice, so influence becomes demonstrable rather than asserted.
- **Name an owner for every finding.** The essay's failure mode — a sound finding with no one accountable — is an org-design defect, and it is fixable by contract.

## Tensions

- **Complements [[wiki/sources/nngroup-accelerating-research-with-ai|NN/g]] precisely.** NN/g maps *what* can be accelerated (planning and analysis, not conducting); Venkat argues *why acceleration alone does not help*. Together: the delegable band is real, narrow, and in the middle — which is exactly the part that was never the bottleneck.
- **Converges with [[wiki/sources/uxr-market-research-data-science-reorg|Papas's reorg thesis]] on the destination, disagrees on the mechanism.** Both see the work moving to interpretation and decision influence. Papas expects disciplines to *merge* under shared state; Venkat expects researchers to move *upstream and downstream* within the existing discipline. Neither addresses the other.
- **Against [[wiki/concepts/ux-research/uxr-role-split|the UXR role-split thesis]] in emphasis:** Newton splits the profession into three roles (Knowledge Makers / Systems Builders / Orchestrators); Venkat treats framing-and-influence as one integrated job. Compatible readings, different unit of analysis.
- **Unresolved headcount question.** Every source in this cluster argues the work moves up the value chain. None addresses whether the same number of people is needed at the top of it.

## Open Questions

- Is there evidence that faster research changes decision quality — in either direction? This cluster asserts it does not and measures nothing.
- What does an organization that reliably assigns ownership to findings actually do differently? A concrete operating model, not a principle.
- If framing and influence are the scarce skills, how are they taught and assessed? Existing UXR training is method-centric.
- Does decision partnering require fewer researchers than study execution did?

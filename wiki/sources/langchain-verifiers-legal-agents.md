# Designing Efficient Verifiers for Legal Agents (LangChain Labs × Harvey)

## Summary

A joint LangChain Labs + Harvey study on making LLM **verifiers** — the LLM-as-judge components that score an agent's output pass/fail against rubric criteria — cheaper while staying near frontier-model accuracy, for both **evals** and **RL post-training** of legal agents. Legal work is hard for agents (many context-filling documents, specialized knowledge, strict acceptance criteria), and Harvey's LAB benchmark verifies like a human reviewer: each task carries a set of **criteria**, each judged by its own LLM call that gets the agent output + a `match_criteria` and emits a `verdict`. With 50+ criteria per task, one frontier call per criterion is expensive at scale.

The team tests two levers — **fewer tokens** (batch scoring: one judge call labels the whole rubric vs one call per criterion) and **cheaper tokens** (smaller/open verifier models) — using Opus 4.7 per-criterion as the reference baseline against GPT-5.5, Sonnet 4.6, Haiku 4.5, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Over 40 public LAB tasks (2,348 rubric criteria, generated by a `Kimi K2.6` agent), they find verifier cost can drop an **order of magnitude** via batching + open models, with **DeepSeek** a strong, far cheaper Opus approximation (60–1000x cheaper at scale) and **Haiku** too permissive for legal (48.4% per-criterion false-pass). A final lever — auto-research-loop **prompt tuning** on trace divergences — further cuts false-pass rates. This is the wiki's clearest worked example of verifier/LLM-as-judge cost-engineering, complementing the eval-pyramid framing in [[sources/bayer-prince-reliable-agentic-ai|Bayer PRINCE]] (RAGAS) and [[sources/vibe-coding-agent-security-evaluation-day-4|Day 4 Security & Evaluation]] ("worth shipping").

## Key Claims

- **Verifiers are a cost bottleneck** for agent evals and RL post-training at scale; post-training amplifies cost via multiple rollouts per task. Cheaper reward signals make it practical to run more experiments and iterate faster.
- **Two efficiency levers:** fewer tokens (batch scoring) and cheaper tokens (smaller/open models). They are largely independent and stack.
- **Per-criterion vs batch:** per-criterion = one judge call per requirement (narrow decision window, many calls, higher accuracy); batch = one call labeling every requirement at once (an order of magnitude cheaper for the same model by saving repeated input-token cost, but lower match rate because the judge must track the full rubric).
- **Setup:** 40 public LAB tasks (Corporate M&A, Tax, Emerging Companies/VC, Trusts & Estates), 2,348 pass/fail rubric criteria, agent outputs from `Kimi K2.6`; Opus-4.7 per-criterion is the baseline all others are scored against.
- **Metrics:** Agreement, False pass, False fail, Cost — with deliberate focus on **false pass** as the dangerous failure mode in legal (a false *fail* can be escalated for review; a false *pass* slips through).
- **Frontier models disagree:** GPT-5.5 vs Opus reach only **95.7% match**, implying ~95.7% is a realistic upper bound (some criteria under-specified even for experts), not 100%.
- **DeepSeek-V4-Flash ≈ Opus** as a verifier in both modes, runnable **3 orders of magnitude (60–1000x) cheaper** → ideal for large-data / RL-training verification at scale, especially where tasks aren't programmatically verifiable.
- **Haiku 4.5 is cheap but too permissive:** false-pass **48.4% per-criterion / 34.7% batch** — wrong failure mode for legal.
- **Verification is a performance/cost/time tradeoff**, like most agent-system design.
- **Prompt tuning works:** an auto-research loop diagnosed that DeepSeek passed criteria *related* to but not fully satisfying a requirement; the tuned prompt forced explicit per-clause checklist decomposition + caution under unclear evidence, cutting DeepSeek false-pass **10.7%→9.5%** (per-criterion) and **15.6%→14.2%** (batch).
- **Open models enable bespoke fine-tuned verifiers**, challenging the assumption that closed frontier models are the gold standard to distill toward (they themselves disagree on ~4-5% of labels).

## Useful Examples

- **The LAB criterion-as-rubric verification model** — task → set of criteria → per-criterion LLM judge (`match_criteria` in, `verdict` out). A reusable template for any rubric-based agent eval, directly analogous to an Eval Plan's pass/fail checks (cf. [[sources/yozm-ai-prd|Yozm AI PRD]] Eval Plan).
- **Batch vs per-criterion as a tunable knob** — a concrete, copyable way to trade ~accuracy for ~10x cost.
- **DeepSeek-as-cheap-verifier** — a worked cost/accuracy result: near-Opus agreement at orders-of-magnitude lower cost, the practical enabler for at-scale RL verification.
- **False-pass as the metric that matters in high-stakes domains** — reframes "agreement" into asymmetric-cost terms (escalate a false fail; fear a false pass).
- **Auto-research prompt-tuning loop on trace divergences** — mine where a cheap verifier diverges from the reference, then distill that behavior back via prompt (checklist decomposition). A transferable verifier-improvement recipe.
- **95.7% inter-frontier-model agreement as a realistic ceiling** — a useful sanity check against chasing 100% eval agreement.

## Constraints / Caveats

- **Vendor engineering blog** (LangChain Labs + Harvey, both with commercial stakes in agent evaluation/LangSmith and legal agents). Confidence **0.8**; treat the method as credible, the framing as vendor-lens.
- **Single domain (legal) + single benchmark (LAB) + single agent (`Kimi K2.6`).** The 40-task / 2,348-criterion result may not generalize to other domains, rubrics, or agents.
- **Specific numbers are setup-specific:** the false-pass rates, 95.7% match, and 60–1000x cost ratios are tied to these models/tasks/prompts as of mid-2026 model versions; re-measure before relying on them.
- **Opus-as-baseline is a proxy for ground truth, not ground truth** — the post explicitly notes even Opus disagrees with GPT-5.5 ~4.3% of the time; "agreement with Opus" is not the same as "correctness."
- **Cost figures are observed token cost extrapolations,** not audited bills; provider prices change.

## Design Implications

- **Treat the verifier as a first-class, separately-engineered component** of any eval/RL pipeline — choose its model and scoring mode by cost/accuracy/risk, not by defaulting to the most expensive frontier model.
- **Pick the verifier model by failure-mode asymmetry,** not raw agreement: in high-stakes domains optimize against **false pass** even at some accuracy/cost cost; avoid permissive cheap models (Haiku-style) there.
- **Use batch scoring to cut eval/training cost ~10x** where a modest match-rate drop is acceptable; reserve per-criterion for the highest-stakes checks.
- **Use open models (DeepSeek-class) for at-scale verification** (large datasets, RL rollouts) and consider **fine-tuning bespoke verifiers** for crucial domains rather than assuming closed-frontier is the ceiling.
- **Tune verifier prompts from divergence traces** (checklist decomposition, caution under ambiguity) — a cheap lever before reaching for bigger models.
- **For an [[concepts/ux-research/ai-evals|AI evals]] / Eval-Plan practice:** set a realistic agreement ceiling (~95% inter-model), define pass/fail criteria tightly, and budget verifier cost explicitly.

## Tensions

- **Accuracy vs cost vs time** — the central tradeoff: per-criterion is most accurate but many calls; batch is cheap/fast but drifts; cheap models save cost but risk permissiveness.
- **False pass vs false fail** — symmetric "agreement" hides asymmetric real-world cost; the right verifier minimizes the *expensive* error, not total disagreement.
- **Closed-frontier-as-gold-standard vs open-model reality** — the post pushes against distilling toward closed models when those models themselves disagree ~4-5% (echoes the model-neutrality argument in [[sources/langchain-model-neutrality|model neutrality]]).
- **Tighter rubrics vs realistic ceilings** — some disagreement is under-specified criteria, not model error; chasing 100% may be chasing noise.
- **Cheaper verification enabling more RL vs over-trusting a cheap reward signal** — cheaper reward signals invite more training, but a permissive verifier can teach the wrong behavior.

## Open Questions

- How should "false pass" tolerance be set per criterion when even the Opus baseline is imperfect?
- What is the downstream effect on the trained agent of verifying with a cheaper (slightly noisier) reward signal?

# UX Research as a Service

**Authors:** Trevor Calabro
**Published:** 2026-06-13 — UX Research in the Wild (Substack)

## Summary

A practitioner essay arguing that UX research should adopt a professional **service model** — but explicitly *not* the weak sense of order-taking or waiting for study requests. Calabro distinguishes order-taking (passive, reactive) from a service in the professional sense: one that has judgment, standards, expertise, and responsibility for helping the people around it get better outcomes, and that helps *clarify what is actually needed* rather than just fulfilling requests. He frames this as closer to **servant leadership** than order-taking. The essay is positioned as the long-delayed clearer restatement of his earlier "Opt-In Approach" philosophy (written ~2.5 years prior), which had been misunderstood.

The central argument: service does **not** make research less strategic — it makes it **more** strategic, because it puts research closer to the decisions that shape the product. Over 20+ years, the most influential researchers are the ones useful *throughout* the work, close enough to see how decisions form, where assumptions harden, and where evidence would change direction. Influence comes from being involved early enough to shape the questions the team asks, not from waiting for a formal study request. Importantly, this does not mean more research on every project — a service mindset should not make the process heavier; it should help the team spend its learning effort where it matters most.

Calabro then walks the model across the product lifecycle: (1) **Shaping** — research should start before there's a prototype or agreed problem; researchers help name the riskiest assumptions before they become requirements, surfacing user uncertainty before it gets buried. (2) **Pairing closely with UX design/strategy** — as ideas become workflows/screens/requirements, researchers help make assumptions visible; contributions range from a formal study to a lightweight study, reviewing prior findings, a working session, reframing a preference debate, or recommending the team move forward because evidence is already strong enough. (3) **Helping Product see uncertainty earlier** — sorting user-uncertainty questions into "answer before design moves," "explore during options," "prototype testing," "post-launch measurement," and "interesting but not important enough to slow the project." (4) **Prototype, testing, build** — ensuring the prototype can answer the team's real questions; a stakeholder-review prototype differs from a user-testing prototype; evaluative studies are stronger because the embedded researcher knows constraints and what can still change, connecting findings to actual project choices. (5) **Post-launch** — staying in to learn whether the work actually helped (workflow ease, support tickets, adoption, whether a problem moved, whether the goal was met), closing the loop for the next project.

Conclusion: UX research can help software teams move through uncertainty with more honesty and less waste — starting in shaping, pairing with UX design/strategy, supporting Product, informing testing, staying through build, and learning after launch. This is the opposite of the order-taker / too-removed / low-autonomy department. Insights and influence matter but are incomplete without *usefulness*; service is the frame that makes research most strategic.

## Key Points

- **Service ≠ order-taking.** "Service" in the professional sense has judgment, standards, expertise, and responsibility; it clarifies what's actually needed instead of just fulfilling requests. Closer to **servant leadership**.
- Two unhelpful drift patterns to avoid: research that is *too removed* (appears only for a formal study) and research that is *too reactive* (responds to the next incoming request). Neither fits real product work where decisions are made before a research plan exists.
- **Service makes research MORE strategic** by putting it closer to decisions. Most-influential researchers are useful throughout, see assumptions hardening, and shape the questions early. Influence grows organically from delivering real-world results.
- Strategic research need not be big/formal/visible/removed — often the opposite: it's close to real decisions, the people making them, and the moments uncertainty could misdirect work.
- A service mindset should **not make the process heavier**; help the team spend learning effort where it matters most. Not every project needs more research.
- **Service starts during shaping** (before a prototype, before the direction hardens, sometimes before the problem is agreed). Product, UX strategy, UX design, and UX research should work together. Research makes *user uncertainty* visible before it gets buried in requirements.
- Shaping questions a researcher helps answer: Which problem are we actually solving? Who's affected? Which decisions are already made? Where is there still room to change direction? What evidence already exists (past research, secondary research, analytics, support tickets, sales feedback, prior usability issues)? Which assumptions are carried forward only for momentum?
- A researcher helps **name the riskiest assumptions before they become requirements.** Not every unknown needs a study, but every project benefits from knowing which unknowns matter.
- **Pairing closely with UXers** post-shaping: assumptions show up inside the actual experience (a vague idea → workflow; a goal → screen; a loose requirement → hierarchy/sequence/content/layout/interaction decisions). The researcher helps make those assumptions easier to see.
- Researcher contributions menu: run a formal study (high risk); run a lightweight study (without changing pace); review prior findings; hold a quick working session to pressure-test a workflow; reframe a preference debate around usability/behavior/risk; **recommend moving forward because existing evidence is already strong enough.**
- **"We already have enough evidence to make this decision" is a valid, under-discussed research contribution.** The researcher isn't trying to own the UX decision — they help others decide with better information and fewer hidden assumptions.
- **Help Product see uncertainty earlier.** User uncertainty (unclear primary users distort scope; competing workflows create sequencing problems; weak concept understanding affects design+dev; low confidence the new experience beats the old affects launch) is as disruptive as scope/timeline uncertainty. Sort questions: answer before design moves / explore during options / prototype testing (interaction-level) / post-launch measurement (adoption, behavior) / interesting-but-not-important.
- **Prototype/testing/build:** ensure the prototype can answer real questions; a stakeholder-review prototype ≠ a user-testing prototype (needs realism around task, context, decision points, not perfection). Embedded researchers produce stronger evaluative studies and connect findings to actual choices (fix before release / content change vs. structural redesign / rethink the flow on model mismatch / monitor a small issue after launch).
- **Post-launch:** stay close enough for findings to influence decisions; learn whether the work helped (workflow ease, support-ticket changes, adoption, whether a problem moved, whether the original goal was addressed). Closes the loop; gives the next project a stronger start; research becomes organizational memory.
- Relates to and restates his prior **"Opt-In Approach"** philosophy (and its real-world implementation follow-up).

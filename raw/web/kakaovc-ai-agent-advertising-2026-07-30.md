## Summary

The argument: search-era advertising occupied *space* in a results page the user could compare; AI-agent advertising infiltrates the *judgment-formation process itself*. That relocates the competitive battleground from information access to **trustworthy delegation**, and it creates a textbook **principal–agent problem** — the agent's commercial incentives may diverge from the principal's interests.

The load-bearing evidence is a cited Princeton study showing that sponsored recommendations delivered conversationally are far more effective than search ads, and that disclosure barely dents them.

## Analytical framework — the four-tier delegation spectrum

Measured by how much judgment the user cedes:

| Tier | Description | Commercial-influence risk |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Filtered search** | Minimal AI intervention | Lowest |
| 2. **Comparative recommendations** | AI suggests options; user chooses | ↑ |
| 3. **Direct recommendations** | AI picks the "best" option; user rarely compares | ↑↑ |
| 4. **Autonomous execution** | AI implements the decision; user sees results only | Highest |

Risk *and* opportunity for commercial influence escalate together across the tiers.

## Key claims

- **Advertising changes in kind, not degree.** From occupying space in a results page to shaping how the judgment is formed.
- **Disclosure alone is insufficient in AI contexts.** Labels and warnings barely reduced the effect (see numbers below).
- **Restructure incentives, don't just disclose them.** The insurance industry's approach — spreading commissions over time so the agent's payoff tracks the client's long-term outcome — is offered as the better model.
- **Trust is a defensible moat, not a compliance cost.** Startup competitive advantage will depend on demonstrating trustworthiness rather than raw model performance.

## Numbers cited (Princeton study, as reported)

| Condition | Sponsored-product selection rate |
|---|---:|
| Conversational AI presenting sponsored products while subtly downplaying alternatives | **61.2%** |
| Search engines, comparable setup | **22.4%** |
| Conversational AI **with explicit "Sponsored" labels and warnings** | **55.5%** |
| User detection rate when intent was hidden | **below 10%** |

The disclosure finding is the sharpest: explicit labelling moved selection only from 61.2% to 55.5%.

## Company examples

- **Anthropic** — rejected advertising entirely; 2026 Super Bowl message quoted as: "Ads are coming to AI. But not to Claude."
- **OpenAI** — launched advertising trials on free and budget ChatGPT tiers, expanding across multiple countries.
- **Amazon Rufus** and **Amazon Buy for Me** — comparative-tier and execution-tier delegation examples.
- **Google Flights**, **Skyscanner** — constrained-parameter search examples (tier 1).

## Anticipated regulatory trajectory (three phases)

1. **Disclosure phase** — labelling sponsored content.
2. **Structure phase** — regulating recommendation mechanisms and performance-based payment contracts.
3. **Authority phase** — restricting what AI may autonomously execute without explicit user approval.

## Investment implications as stated

Founders should build explainable recommendation architectures pre-emptively, audit revenue models for conflicts with user interests, and treat trust as a moat.

## Caveats

- **Forward-looking and partly speculative**, especially the three-phase regulatory trajectory.
- **The Princeton study is cited, not linked or fully characterized here** — no sample size, task design, or publication venue captured. Needs primary verification before external citation.
- **Insurance-industry parallels may not transfer** to digital contexts with much faster decision cycles.

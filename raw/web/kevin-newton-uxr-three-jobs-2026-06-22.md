# UX Research Is Splitting Into Three Jobs

**Author:** Kevin Newton ("Systems Leader | Passionate Storyteller"; UX research / UX measurement leader)
**Published:** 2026-06-18 — LinkedIn
**Capture note:** Full article text supplied by Bonny on 2026-06-22 (the LinkedIn login wall had blocked direct fetch). This replaces the earlier partial stub.

## Summary

Newton argues that for decades UX research has been defined by *methods* (interviews, surveys, usability tests, diary studies), and that definition is breaking — not by choice but because of an economic shift: **the cost of producing research is dropping to near zero.** Transcription, coding, synthesis, tagging, recruitment, survey generation, and first-pass analysis that once took a team weeks now take one person an afternoon (citing a 2024 User Interviews survey: 90% of UX pros already use AI in analysis/synthesis, 73% to summarize transcripts/notes). When production cost collapses, the work doesn't disappear — it moves up the value chain, splitting the discipline into three roles.

## The Three Roles

1. **Knowledge Makers — create understanding.** As AI absorbs mechanical work, the highest-value researchers focus on what machines can't: framing the right question, challenging assumptions, rigorous research and deep analysis, turning noise into a point of view. Relies on *taste* — the practiced ability to recognize "good" (paraphrasing Dave Birss: professionals' taste flags sub-par AI output instantly, but AI also makes novices *feel* competent, so spotting good/bad gets harder). Much current research is really information-gathering (just getting the team in front of five users); when information becomes abundant, that logistics role evaporates and **interpretation becomes the scarce, valuable thing.** They prevent "garbage in, garbage out."
2. **Research Systems Builders — create leverage.** Build the infrastructure that embodies researcher expertise so research can be automated at scale: AI workflows, quality standards, information automation, reusable knowledge. The next expansion of ResearchOps (wrongly filed under "support"). Example: a PM self-serves a clean themed answer from last quarter's study instead of commissioning a redundant one; a designer automates testing + trusted results. Today often a researcher + ops collaboration; tomorrow increasingly one role (ops people learning research and/or researchers learning ops).
3. **Orchestrators — create impact.** The least recognized today, maybe most important tomorrow. In a world where signal comes from everywhere (studies, analytics, support tickets, surveys, experiments, AI agents), someone decides how it combines into a decision. Orchestrators maintain relationships with product teams, know how evidence becomes a decision, and which signals are noise. Canonical move: a PM says "I need a study," the Orchestrator says "You don't — we already know this from three sources; let me pull the support cluster and the experiment readout, you'll have your answer Thursday." They rarely produce research; they make sure the org learns from what it already knows.

## Key Points / Quotes

- "Customers don't care what we learned. Product teams don't care where we learned it. Executives don't care how we learned it. They care whether we helped them make a better decision at the right time to create a better experience."
- "When technology collapses the cost of production, the work doesn't disappear. It moves up the value chain."
- NN/g (2025): "Critical thinking, creativity, and taste — the ability to discern and curate a series of outputs and decisions — will become the differentiators."
- "The biggest risk facing UX research isn't AI. It's continuing to define ourselves by activities that are becoming commodities."
- Framing: from *methods* → *outcomes*; from practitioners of methods → creators, enablers, and orchestrators of knowledge. "This isn't researchers versus AI."

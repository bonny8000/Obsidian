# There Is No Such Thing as a Representative Sample

**Author:** Saeideh Bakhshi — Research Toolbox (Substack)
**Published:** 2026-06-22
**Subtitle:** "A sample only represents the claim it was designed to support."

## Summary

Bakhshi argues that "we need a representative sample" is usually a vibe, not a research design. Demographic quota-matching (age/gender/region/segment) is inherited from opinion polling, where demographics correlate with the outcome and population margins are known. **Product research is different:** the outcomes we care about (task completion, onboarding success, adoption, retention, willingness to pay, error recovery, trust after failure) are driven by behavioral/contextual variables (intent, tenure, plan tier, prior experience, workflow complexity, integration depth, role, stakes, job-to-be-done), not demographics. A sample is **not representative in the abstract — it is only more or less adequate for a specific claim about a specific population.**

## Key Points

- Separate the layers: **target population** (who the claim is about) → **sample frame** (who you could reach) → **realized sample** (who was selected) → **respondents** (who participated). Every gap can distort a specific claim.
- **Four claims, four sampling logics:**
  - **Prevalence** (how common?): needs people not systematically different on the thing being estimated; selecting on the outcome (e.g., surveying only onboarding *completers*) biases it; more N just gives a precise wrong number.
  - **Existence** (does it happen at all?): one well-documented case can suffice; sample deliberately from tail conditions where the problem appears; the "middle" is the wrong place to look.
  - **Explanatory** (why does it happen?): the sample MUST include people who had the outcome (churned, dropped off); survivors-only gives a coherent-but-wrong explanation.
  - **Comparison** (how do groups differ?): each group must represent its subgroup via *comparable* recruitment; mismatched recruitment (forum power-users vs in-product intercept new-users) confounds the difference.
- **Three ways "representative" fails:** (1) balanced on the wrong (easy-to-quota) variables; (2) selected by the outcome (nonresponse/self-selection/survivorship bias) — more N doesn't fix bias; (3) changing the claim after sampling (a detection/existence study read as a frequency study = category error; "we observed" silently becomes "most users").
- **Design the sample backward from the claim.** Ask: who is the claim about? who could recruiting reach? who's missing in a way that changes the answer? what claim type? balanced on outcome-predicting variables or just quota-easy ones? is the sample available *because of* the outcome?
- Punchline: "Representative of what? For the purpose of claiming what?" Until answered, "representative" is just a vibe.

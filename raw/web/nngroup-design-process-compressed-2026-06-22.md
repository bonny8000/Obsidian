# Design Process Isn't Dead, It's Compressed

**Authors:** Sarah Gibbons, Huei-Hsin Wang
**Published:** 2026-03-13 — Nielsen Norman Group

## Summary

The article responds to a popular "throw out the process, trust your intuition, skip the research, start building" argument in design discourse (associated most prominently with Jenny Wen, design lead at Anthropic). NN/G's counter is that this argument misrepresents how experienced designers actually work. What looks like "skipping the process" is really **process compression**: experts run an internalized version of discovery, ideation, and evaluation faster and nonlinearly, using accumulated experience as a guide. The frameworks (double diamond, design thinking, jobs-to-be-done) were never meant as literal checklists — they are scaffolding for managing risk and making the thinking approachable and transferable.

The piece argues "what gets called intuition is really process, compressed and internalized through years of doing the work." AI tools (vibecoding, AI prototyping) further accelerate this compression *and democratize it* — letting less experienced practitioners run the loop faster too. But intuition does not *replace* process: it is not viable for juniors who lack the accumulated knowledge, it lacks accountability (process artifacts are needed for stakeholder alignment and "what evidence backs this up?"), it fails in regulated/high-stakes domains (healthcare, finance, government, accessibility), and even well-earned intuition carries bias (anchoring on familiar patterns, dismissing edge cases). Solution-first design works only in narrow, mature contexts and reflects survivorship bias (we see the wins, not the failed solution-first experiments). The real skill is **process literacy**: deliberately matching the process to the problem, knowing which approach fits and the risk of skipping it. Speed of execution does not diminish the importance of problem framing.

## Key Points

- The "anti-process" argument: traditional process feels outdated; iteration/intuition/skipping steps are strengths; great work often starts with solutions, not problem statements (you understand the problem only after seeing a compelling prototype).
- These observations are not wrong, but they describe what *experienced* designers already do — they do not prove process is unnecessary.
- **Process compression, not abandonment:** "skipping the process" is compressing it — running internalized stages faster, often within established paradigms and well-tested patterns. The double diamond / design thinking cycle are simplified representations of stages everyone moves through (figure out what's wrong → what to build → build it → learn from it).
- **"What gets called 'intuition' is really process, compressed and internalized through years of doing the work."** The intuition designers trust was built by the very process they dismiss.
- **AI accelerates and democratizes compression:** vibecoding shrinks the gap between idea and testable artifact; less experienced practitioners can now run the design loop fast, like experts always have.
- **Intuition does not replace process — four failure modes:**
  1. *Not everyone can operate on intuition.* Juniors lack the accumulated user/behavior/business knowledge that makes intuition reliable.
  2. *Intuition lacks accountability.* "I followed my intuition" does not survive a VP asking "What evidence backs this up?" Process artifacts (research findings, usability results, analytics) enable stakeholder alignment.
  3. *Intuition fails in regulated/high-stakes industries.* Healthcare, finance, government, accessibility — process is a safeguard against harm, not bureaucratic ritual. Skipping research on a medical-device UI ≠ skipping it on a whiteboarding feature.
  4. *Intuition carries bias.* Anchoring on familiar patterns; dismissing edge cases. The deeper the intuition, the harder the bias is to detect. Process forces assumptions into the open.
- **Solution-first works only in narrow contexts.** Wen's examples (successful features of a well-resourced AI product) reflect survivorship bias — failed solution-first experiments are invisible. Solution-first suits mature product spaces, sophisticated users, discrete differentiation, and high org/UX maturity. In low-maturity/novel/high-stakes contexts it amplifies the cost of wrong assumptions.
- "Speed of execution doesn't diminish the importance of proper problem framing." You may not need a formal problem statement or discovery sprint, but you need to know what you are trying to fix.
- **Process literacy** is the real modern skill: pick the right approach and tool for the problem; know which process fits and the risk of not following it; don't claim you're not using a process when you're just applying it differently. Match the process to the problem deliberately — not because someone declared process dead.
- Closing: as AI compresses more workflow and agentic systems begin to act, remember, and decide on our behalf, skipping the "how does our work map to the problem?" question becomes *more* costly, not less.

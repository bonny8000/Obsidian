# People loved how I described my services. They just didn't want to hire me for it. (Positioning Experiment)

**Author:** Svenja Pieritz (Senior Quantitative UX Researcher; background in cognitive science)
**Published:** 2026-06-08 — second piece in a series on running a positioning study on her own consulting business.

## Summary

A between-subjects framing experiment Pieritz ran on **90 senior decision-makers** (n=30/cell), testing three descriptions of her services and measuring two things: **relevance** ("does this connect to a challenge you face?") and **purchase intent** ("how likely to consider contracting this person?"). The three framings: **person** (leads with her name/background), **method** (leads with the service/offer), **problem** (leads with the buyer's pain point). She was most confident in the **problem** framing.

## Key Points

- **Result:** all three scored similarly on **relevance**; the gap appeared on **purchase intent** — **person framing won, method second, problem framing last** (and not by a trivial margin). The one she was most confident about lost on the only metric that matters.
- **Relevance ≠ buying intent.** People recognized the problem as real, agreed it mattered, and still didn't connect that to a decision to buy — the same "say-do" gap she's seen across projects.
- **Desire ≠ willingness to pay** — illustrated by a prior Kiwi.com extras study: travellers wanted high-value extras, but bundling them with a real price made the package feel expensive and conversion dropped.
- **The decisive evidence was qualitative, not the numbers:** open-ended reactions to the person framing were personal and vivid ("exactly the kind of person you'd want on a product team"); the problem framing drew polite-but-distant replies ("sounds practical", "sounds AI generated… does not read like a person") — one gave 4/5 relevance and 1/5 intent.
- **Lesson:** a real person with a real name/background cuts through in a way no carefully crafted problem framing can; leaving the person out makes you "sound like everything else on the internet." She changed behavior (started writing personal process articles) rather than redesigning the site off one study.
- **Honest methodology note:** n=30/cell, screened for seniority + company size (50+); ANOVA on purchase intent significant (p=.034) but non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis not (p=.054); medium effect (Cohen's f=0.284); underpowered for small-medium effects — "directional finding is clear but the inferential claim is not bulletproof."

## Framing

The term **"minimally technical reporting"** is credited to **Chapman and Rodden**:

> "Good reports are short, focused on action, minimally technical, and unbiased."

## Core thesis

Communication must be calibrated to the audience's level. Junior researchers share exhaustive detail with peers who want it; as the career progresses, stakeholders shift toward executives who cannot absorb granular insight. The skill is knowing how far up the iceberg to surface.

> "Most people don't care about how you got your answer. Some don't care about your answer, they just want to know what they should do next."

## The iceberg — five levels

From submerged to visible: **workings → technical appendix → memo → briefing → headline.** Information density decreases as audience seniority rises.

Author's caveat, stated explicitly: *"These levels are not strict, I can see arguments for fewer or more."*

## Concrete rules given

**Depth limits by audience:**

> "If you show a table to executives, you've gone too deep. If you show a dodged or stacked bar chart to executives, you've gone too far."

> "You're not ready to talk to executives about your work until you can do it with one number per slide maximum"

- Briefing deck: **10–20 slides maximum**
- **Three sentences maximum** per slide
- Executive presentation: **one number per slide maximum, or no numbers**
- Prepare a plain-language explanation for **every** number shown to executives — assume they will question any visible detail; use that expectation as the filter for what to include

**Order of production (stated as a procedure):**

1. Write the technical appendix first — exploratory, exhaustive
2. Write the memo **from scratch as a new document**, rather than editing the appendix down
3. Keep deep links from memo back to appendix
4. Build the deck **from the completed memo**, not the reverse

**Format matching:** decks are for presenting, documents are for reading. A deep-dive memo formatted as a deck is described as *"trying to do two jobs at once while failing at both."* Schedule separate readouts per team rather than combining insights into one presentation.

**Visualization:**

> "The chart that helps you understand the data isn't necessarily the chart that will help your stakeholder understand what you learned"

- Good: single-point bar charts with one bar highlighted; annotated line charts describing the movement; single-insight slides with simple visuals
- Bad for executives: tables; dodged or stacked bar charts; driver-analysis charts with regression-coefficient axes shown without plain-language translation

**Translating statistics:** express results as relative risk in plain language — *"Users that do X are 3x more likely to be satisfied than users who do Y"* — rather than showing coefficients or p-values. Technical machinery named as belonging in the appendix: generalized linear mixed effects models, non-response bias weights, sandwich estimators, regression coefficients, top-2-box percentages. Qualitative equivalents mentioned: affinity mapping, familiarization, mental model mapping (the last noted as not yet used by the author).

## Limitations and caveats (as observed in the text)

- **Explicitly non-prescriptive about the level count** — *"These levels are not strict."*
- **Organization-dependent** — *"The way you implement the details of your UX research communication will be very organization dependent."*
- Exceptions acknowledged to the one-number rule (*"Sure there are exceptions"*).
- **Experience-based, not researched.** Built from the author's own mistakes and successes; no study, no data, no citation beyond the Chapman & Rodden phrase.
- Assumes a **hierarchical organization with executive leadership**; does not address flat organizations.
- Does not address stakeholders who **demand a particular format** regardless of fit.
- Weighted toward **quantitative** UX research, though qualitative methods are mentioned.
- Author reports a personal failure case (*"I stumbled and ultimately failed to give a decent answer"*) as the origin of the framework — candid, but it means the model is a post-hoc rationalization of experience rather than a tested one.
